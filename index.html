Grasping in the Dark with In-Hand Proximity →

In complete darkness, my policy with in-hand time-of-flight (ToF) proximity sensors grasped a hidden Pringles can at all 16 test positions. A camera-only policy, and one that simply took the same sensor readings as an extra input, grasped none.

Preprint

I am a post-master researcher in Prof. Hyung-Soon Park’s lab at KAIST, where I finished my M.S. in mechanical engineering in August 2026. For my thesis, I built seven time-of-flight proximity sensors into a dexterous robot hand and fed three of them into π0.5, a pretrained vision-language-action (VLA) model. The policy at first ignored the new sense, so I changed its inputs and training until it relied on the sensors. Before KAIST, I studied mechanical engineering at UC San Diego and then worked as a full-time mechanical engineer in industry for about three years. During my master’s, I learned how to collect demonstrations and fine-tune VLA models, but I feel I have only scratched the surface. In a PhD, I want to deepen this knowledge and explore the wider field of robot learning, from tactile and force sensing to the latest VLA architectures, world action models and reinforcement learning.

A pipetting robot you can talk to

I built a three-axis gantry with a motorized pipette, controlled with G-code and grbl firmware, and connected it to a local vision-language model (VLM), Qwen3-VL-8B. The operator types one plain-English sentence, and the model turns it into a pipetting plan of tube names and volumes. The robot then runs the plan only after rule-based safety checks and the operator’s approval.

Natural language to robot motion The local VLM writes a checked protocol, and the executor turns it into G-code.

  • 6 of 8 test sentences became the correct protocol in one try each, including one in Korean. Rule-based checks now stop the other two before the robot moves.
  • 12 steps from one sentence ran on the robot: moves, transfers, a mix and a tip change.
  • 477–589 s per run over 3 runs; the two longer runs include fitting the first tip.

Calibrated custom pipette Water weighed on a 0.001 g balance, with the pipette hand-held off the gantry.

  • 13.0 µL/mm: volume moved per millimetre of plunger travel.
  • 0.1–2.5%: error of the mean volume against the calibration’s prediction.
  • 1.55–2.86%: shot-to-shot spread (coefficient of variation) at 96–200 µL.

Safe by design

  • The executor makes the coordinates: it converts the model’s plan (tube names, volumes and order) into positions from the saved tube positions and safety rules such as a safe Z height, so the pipette avoids the parts on the deck.
  • Tip-volume check: impossible protocols, such as dispensing more than the tip holds, are rejected before they run.
  • Operator approval: the robot moves only after the operator approves the plan.
