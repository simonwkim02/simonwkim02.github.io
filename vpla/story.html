Grasping in the dark

How I gave a robot hand a sense of proximity and got a vision-language-action policy to use it, from September 2025 to November 2026.

In short

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies map camera images and a written instruction to robot motion. I built time-of-flight (ToF) proximity sensors into a dexterous robot hand and made a VLA policy use this new sense.

In complete darkness, this vision-proximity-language-action (VPLA) policy approached, grasped and pulled out a Pringles can hidden in a shelf at 16 of 16 new positions. A camera-only policy and a control whose proximity input carries no information never grasped it.
Blinded, pre-registered robot test, November 13, 2026. One object, one training run per policy, test positions within the demonstrated area.

Sep–Dec 2025

Looking for a problem worth a thesis

My first thesis idea, a haptic teleoperation system for collecting robot demonstrations, was too large for a master's thesis and not new enough. I therefore learned VLA training end to end at small scale on two low-cost SO-101 arms I assembled, recording bolt-into-nut demonstrations, and found that a thread slightly off can only be felt, not seen.

This raised the question the rest of the project answers: which sense besides vision should a VLA get?

Jan 2026

Why proximity, and a visit to KIST

For VLA models, proximity sensing (distance before contact) was almost untouched, unlike touch. My lab's P2GI paper (2024) used palm proximity sensors on a prosthetic hand to choose a grasp, though without a VLA model. On January 2, I visited its first author, Dr. Si-Hwan Heo, at the Korea Institute of Science and Technology (KIST), who advised me to start small, show a clear gain over a camera-only VLA and pick tasks the camera cannot see.

P2GI paper

My first test aimed an 8×8-zone ToF sensor, which returns a grid of 64 distances, at 3D-printed cable ports, which appeared only as blobs. Thus, the sensor cannot compete with cameras at recognizing shapes, but it suits near-contact control, which needs only a rough sense of where things are. I then switched to the TMF8829 ToF chip, which measures up to a 48×32 grid of distances (16×16 in this work) and fits in a fingertip.

The project became: give a multi-finger hand proximity sensing for the last centimeters before contact, and make a VLA use it.

Feb 27, 2026

The thesis proposal

At my thesis proposal (committee: Profs. Hyung-Soon Park, Jung Kim and Jemin Hwangbo), I committed to ToF sensors in a Tesollo DG-5F hand, a glove-and-tracker teleoperation system and a VLA that reads the sensors, compared against the same VLA with vision only.

Mar–Jun 2026

Building a hand that can sense

Circuit boards. To fit the sensor chip in a fingertip, I designed a 12×12 mm four-layer sensor board in KiCad and an interface board to the microcontrollers, and I assembled the second version myself.

Fingertips. The sensor needs a clear cover with no air gap, so I cast clear silicone (PDMS) in 3D-printed molds. However, the resin molds left a cloudy, under-cured surface that reflected the sensor's own light, so after four revisions my final design leaves each sensor window open to the air (steps below).

Wiring and calibration. Two sensors sit on each of the thumb, index and middle fingers and one in the palm, read by two microcontroller boards that run in parallel, each reading its sensors in turn. A per-zone calibration on a motorized stage cut the mean error of 100-frame median readings over the central 8×8 zones from 19.7 to 0.35 mm at three distances left out of the fit (37, 75 and 225 mm).

Apr–Jul 2026

Teleoperation, and the defense

To record demonstrations of the arm and all five fingers, I built a leader-follower teleoperation system: a MANUS data glove drives the fingers and a VIVE tracker on the wrist drives the arm. Problems showed up only once the robot was moving, so I calibrated the tracker's axes and smoothed jittery targets with a motion planner that limits sudden changes in acceleration.

I defended my master's thesis on June 22 with a system that worked end to end, but my first VPLA policies ignored the proximity readings, so I had not yet shown that the sensors made a policy better. Slipping grasps also revealed a uniformly light grip in every demonstration, so I recalibrated the finger mapping and added automatic quality checks that reject a bad demonstration as soon as it is recorded.

Aug–Oct 2026

A new arm, a hidden bug, and a task that was too easy

In August I moved the hand to a Fairino FR10 arm and set up a chopstick task: grasp a chopstick standing in one of nine sockets and move it to a white container. Early on, the policies failed on this task without visible cause.

I traced the failures to two bugs in how the robot carried out the policy’s plans. The robot sends the arm one target per 33 ms step (30 Hz), and the policy plans 16 targets at a time.

  • Each plan started in the past. Before the August fix, every switch between plans went like this:
    1. The robot plays targets 1–8 of plan A (0.27 s).
    2. At step t, it captures the camera images and joint angles and asks for plan B, whose target 1 is the arm position for step t.
    3. While plan B is computed (about 0.12 s, or 3–4 steps), the robot plays plan A’s targets 9–11.
    4. Plan B arrives at step t+3 or t+4, so its first 3–4 targets are already in the past.
    5. The robot still started at target 1, so at every switch the arm lurched back by about 0.1 s of motion.
    In August, I made the robot start each plan at the target for the current step.
  • The joint angles were out of date. The FR10 reached each commanded position about 0.18 s, or 5–6 steps, late. The measured joint angles in each snapshot therefore showed where the arm had been sent 5–6 steps earlier. In the demonstrations, however, the recorded angles always equalled the last command, so the policy planned from a stale position. Thus, for a snapshot taken at step t, the measured angles matched the command from step t−5 or t−6, while the plan built from them arrived at step t+3 or t+4. This slowed the approach and shifted where the hand closed. In September, I fed the policy the commanded angles instead.

The second fix raised the camera-only policy from 0 of 6 placements to 4 of 5. It is what first made the chopstick task work, and I discarded every hardware comparison recorded before it.

At first, I trained the camera-only policy on 154 demonstrations in which the arm reached each socket along nearly the same path. It placed the chopstick in only 7 of 28 trials at the seven trained sockets and in none of 8 at the two sockets left out of training, since it had memorized the arm paths. I therefore recorded corrective demonstrations, in which I stop the hand off target on purpose and then fix the grasp by sliding sideways or re-grasping. With 116 of them among 198 training demonstrations and a re-aimed scene camera, the policy placed the chopstick in 18 of 28 trials. However, it still placed none of 8 at the untrained sockets. In its typical miss, the hand stopped about 1 cm short of where successful grasps ended, and the palm proximity sensor measured this offset. Yet policies that also read the proximity sensors never beat the camera-only policy, and the wrist camera saw the chopstick at the moment of grasping in every case I checked.

Oct 2026

Moving the object after the cameras saw it

A camera-only VLA policy already handled the chopstick task, so I built a task in which the object moves after the cameras have seen it. A magnet on a motorized gantry under a felt board drags a small white puck 50–80 mm sideways beneath the hovering hand. I hoped the fingers would hide the puck from the cameras and leave only the palm’s ToF sensor to find it.

However, in a teleoperated pilot on October 22 the fingers never hid the puck. The recorded images of the wrist camera and of a small camera in the palm, checked at 5 Hz through all 14 moves, showed the puck in all 323 frames. In contrast, two of the three 80 mm moves carried the puck out of the ToF sensor’s field of view, yet only to the edge of the palm camera’s image.

The reason lies in how the palm camera is mounted: it is aimed with the palm ToF sensor, and its image contains the sensor’s whole field of view. Thus, in normal light the palm camera sees everything the sensor sees, so no larger move or other direction could make the sensor necessary. Cameras in the fingertips, as some recent robot hands have, would probably see the puck as well.

Therefore, I dropped this task before training any policy on it. Only darkness hides the object from every camera while the ToF sensor keeps working on its own infrared light, so I moved the task into a dark, curtained shelf.

Oct–Nov 6, 2026

A task only proximity can solve

I built a dark task: in a curtained bookshelf in complete darkness, the robot must find, grasp and pull out a Pringles can standing at one of many spots. The same gantry, now under the shelf's felt floor, moves the Pringles can with magnets to a logged position before each trial, accurate to within 4.2 mm in a camera check of 30 positions.

On November 3, the wrist tracker lost tracking, and the arm jumped and hit the shelf, breaking two fingertip mounts. I therefore wrote a safety guard that stops the arm whenever tracking is lost, tested it the next day and recorded a fresh set, keeping 54 demonstrations that each start with the arm at the same pose inside the shelf.

The palm sensor and the two palm-facing fingertip sensors feed the pretrained VLA model π0.5 through a separate sensor network that shares attention with the rest of the model. Offline analysis showed that the policy still ignored them, likely through a shortcut: the fingers began each demonstration at slightly different angles, so the policy could recognize it by its finger joint angles and replay it.

The demonstrations offered an easier answer than the sensor, so I had to take that answer away.

Nov 6–13, 2026

Making the policy use the sensor

To check offline whether a policy uses the sensor, I swapped in proximity readings recorded with the Pringles can elsewhere and measured whether the planned motion shifts toward the new spot. Guided by this test, two training changes made the policy use the sensor:

  • I removed the joint angles from the policy's input, which took away the shortcut.
  • I pre-trained and then froze the sensor encoder, the network that turns the ToF readings into tokens for the model, teaching it to locate the Pringles can at 201 positions set by the gantry.

After policies trained on the first 54 demonstrations missed the Pringles can at the front corners in pilot tests, I recorded 90 more, most at the edges and corners, for 144 training demonstrations in total. A blinded test on November 8 also showed that my control was unfair: with its sensor blanked, the policy closed its hand at once and backed out of the shelf instead of reaching for the Pringles can. As my advisor, Prof. Hyung-Soon Park, pointed out, the control had to get a fair chance at the task before the comparison could show that the ToF sensing makes the difference. The control therefore became a policy trained from the start on proximity readings marked invalid, so any difference could come only from the sensor.

On November 13 I ran the main test, pre-registered and blinded: the three policies ran in random order under code names that a script hid from me, and whether the hand approached the Pringles can, coming down within 40 mm of it, was computed from the robot’s joint logs by a fixed rule. Their motions still gave the policies away, so only this approach score is fully blind. The VPLA policy approached, grasped and pulled out the Pringles can at all 16 new positions, whereas the control approached it once and the camera-only policy three times, the number expected by chance, neither grasping it. A day later, the same model trained without my two changes approached the Pringles can at 0 of 16 positions in a pre-registered but unblinded test.

Next

What comes next

The paper is to be submitted to Robotics: Science and Systems (RSS) 2027, and the preprint goes on arXiv in late November 2026. Its lesson is that, in this task, a pretrained VLA used a sense it was never trained on only after I measured whether it used that sense, removed the joint-angle shortcut and pre-trained its sensor encoder.
